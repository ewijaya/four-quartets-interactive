The poem begins with the river of Eliot's childhood, the Mississippi at St Louis. Speaking casually, the poet calls it a strong brown god: sullen and untamed, first a frontier, then a highway for trade, then merely an engineering problem for bridge-builders. Once the bridges are built, city-dwellers who worship machines almost forget it, but the river keeps its seasons and its floods, destroying and reminding. Its rhythm ran through the poet's childhood: in the nursery, in the tree in the April yard, in the smell of grapes in autumn, in the family circle under the gaslight in winter.
