The last quartet opens with the only long description of a natural scene in the four poems, all of it in the present tense. A day in deep winter feels like a season of its own, everlasting yet waterlogged towards evening, hung in time between the poles and the tropics. When the short day is at its brightest, the low sun sets the ice on ponds and ditches ablaze. Its glare blinds, and a glow fiercer than any fire of branches stirs the silent spirit: no wind, but the fire of Pentecost. Between thaw and frost the soul's sap trembles. This is a kind of spring, but outside the order of the seasons that God promised Noah after the Flood: for an hour the hedges are white with a blossom of snow that neither buds nor fades. Where, the poet asks, is the summer beyond imagining?
