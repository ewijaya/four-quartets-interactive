The rose-garden is reached through a passage not taken and a door never opened: the life that might have been. Biographers have connected this with Eliot's relationship with Emily Hale, with whom he visited Burnt Norton in 1934 — a connection given new weight by his letters to her, opened at Princeton in 2020. The poem itself keeps the experience impersonal: ‘our’ first world, not his.

Commentators have also heard literary echoes: the little door into the lovely garden in Lewis Carroll's *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland*, and Rudyard Kipling's story ‘They’ (1904), where the ghosts of children hide in an English country garden. These are possibilities, not documented sources.
