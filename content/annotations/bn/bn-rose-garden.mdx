The rose-garden is reached through a passage not taken and a door never opened: the life that might have been. Biographers have connected this with Eliot's relationship with Emily Hale, with whom he visited Burnt Norton in 1934 — a connection given new weight by his letters to her, opened at Princeton in 2020. The poem itself keeps the experience impersonal: ‘our’ first world, not his.

Eliot himself named literary sources. In 1941 he told John Hayward of three: his own short poem ‘New Hampshire’, with its children's voices in an orchard; Elizabeth Barrett Browning's ‘The Lost Bower’, about a child who finds a magical bower in a wood and can never find it again; and Rudyard Kipling's story ‘They’ (1904), where the ghosts of children hide in an English country garden. In 1953 he added the beginning of Lewis Carroll's *Alice's Adventures in Wonderland*, where Alice finds the little door into the loveliest garden and cannot get through it.
