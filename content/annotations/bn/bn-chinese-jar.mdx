A Chinese jar is a made thing whose form holds still while it seems to move — the curve carries the eye around it forever. Readers naturally hear Keats's ‘Ode on a Grecian Urn’, already echoed in the first movement's unheard music, but Eliot said in 1962 that he had not been thinking of Keats: his jar came from the International Exhibition of Chinese Art at the Royal Academy in London in the winter of 1935–36, while he was finishing the poem.

‘Still’ is doing double work, as often in the sequence: motionless, and also continuing, even now. The pun is the whole idea in one word: stillness that persists through time.
