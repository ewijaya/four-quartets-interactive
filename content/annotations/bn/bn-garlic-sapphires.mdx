Each second movement opens with a strict lyric, which is then followed by a looser, discursive passage. Burnt Norton's lyric is in short, rhymed lines with a strong beat, closer to incantation than to argument. Its images join the lowest things (garlic, mud, the blood and lymph of the body) to the highest (jewels, stars), and end in reconciliation.

The opening lines were first written as the ending of drafts of another poem, ‘Lines for an Old Man’, and the first word was ‘Thunder’. Eliot confirmed that the image came from a late sonnet by Mallarmé, ‘M'introduire dans ton histoire’, which ends with thunder and rubies at the hubs of a chariot's wheels. Eliot's jewels clog an axle instead — a wheel, and so a turning world, brought to a stop.
