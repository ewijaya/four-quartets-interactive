A house may give way to a field as easily as to a factory or a road. Virginia Woolf, in her 1937 broadcast ‘Craftsmanship’, had remarked how buildings fall and a cornfield turns into a bungalow; Tennyson's ‘Aylmer's Field’ ends with a great house pulled down and its land open field again. Eliot considered ‘playing field’ and rejected it. The phrase also recalls the medieval open-field system, in which a village farmed its land in unfenced strips.

The field matters to the whole movement: the light falls across it in the second paragraph, and in the third it is the place where the village's dead come back to dance.
