Death's chill climbs from the feet to the knees, as it does in Mistress Quickly's account of Falstaff's death in *Henry V*, where she feels him grow cold as stone from the feet upward. The paradox that follows, of freezing in fire, is old too: a character in Webster sweats in ice and freezes in fire. Eliot noted that the stanza was modelled on Edward Benlowes, the seventeenth-century devotional poet, whose *Theophila* has blood freezing to the ice of death. These fires are purgatorial: they purify, as Dante's do.
