The vision has several sources. After a visit in 1936 Eliot wrote that East Coker had given him a feeling like that of *Germelshausen*, and in 1940 he said the imagery of this movement might owe something to his memory of it. The story, by Friedrich Gerstäcker (1860), was a staple of American school readers in German: a young artist falls in love with a girl from a village that appears for only one day in every hundred years, and loses her when it vanishes at midnight. What stayed with Eliot, Gardner suggests, was the idea of a stranger coming upon the revels of the long dead. Gardner notes that the Ordnance Survey map marks an ancient dancing circle near the village. Kipling's ‘The Way Through the Woods’, where on a summer evening you may hear a horse and rider on a road closed seventy years before, has the same hush; so has Milton's belated peasant in *Paradise Lost*, who sees, or dreams he sees, the fairies dancing by moonlight.

Unlike Germelshausen, East Coker is under no curse. The dancers are simply the village's dead, and keeping your distance is the condition of seeing them at all.
