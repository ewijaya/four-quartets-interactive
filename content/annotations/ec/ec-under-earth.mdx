The dancers' mirth is the mirth of the dead, whose bodies now feed the corn. Writing on Kipling in 1941, Eliot admired his feeling for the generations of the dead in the English soil, for a people rooted in its land. The thought returns at the end of *The Dry Salvages*, where the poet hopes that his own body, not far from the churchyard yew, will nourish significant soil. The churchyard he had in mind was East Coker's.
