The poem asks whether the calm and wisdom promised to old age were a deception — whether the elders who seemed serene were only dulled, and what they handed on was a recipe for self-deceit. ‘Hebetude’ is a rare word for mental dullness or lethargy, from the Latin for blunt.

The charge widens into a claim about experience itself: knowledge derived from the past imposes a pattern on the present and so falsifies it, because each moment revalues everything that came before. Ricks and McCue recall the elders of the apocryphal History of Susanna, respected judges who turned away their eyes from heaven and bore false witness. Eliot was fifty-one when the poem appeared. The question of what age brings returns, more terribly, in the ghost's speech in Little Gidding II.
