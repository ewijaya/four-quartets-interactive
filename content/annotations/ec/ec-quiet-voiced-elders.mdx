The poem asks whether the calm and wisdom promised to old age were a deception — whether the elders who seemed serene were only dulled, and what they handed on was a recipe for self-deceit. ‘Hebetude’ is a rare word for mental dullness or lethargy, from the Latin for blunt.

The charge widens into a claim about experience itself: knowledge derived from the past imposes a pattern on the present and so falsifies it, because each moment revalues everything that came before. Eliot was fifty-one when the poem appeared. The question of what age brings returns, more terribly, in the ghost's speech in Little Gidding II.
